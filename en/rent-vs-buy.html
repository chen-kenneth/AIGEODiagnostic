Renting vs Buying: How to Do the Math (Ottawa Edition)

Haiyun Wang · Ottawa Real Estate Agent · 6 min read

"My rent is $2,800 a month, and a similar place costs $650K to buy — which is the better deal?" It's one of the questions I hear most. Let's do the math properly.

Step 1: The True Monthly Cost of Buying

Most people only look at the mortgage payment. That's the biggest mistake. The true cost of ownership is much more:

Price (townhouse example)$650,000
Down payment (20%)$130,000
Mortgage (4.5%, 25-yr)≈ $2,890/mo
Property tax≈ $400/mo
Home insurance≈ $100/mo
Maintenance (1%/yr)≈ $540/mo
True monthly cost≈ $3,930

* Illustrative example — actual numbers vary by price, rate and taxes. Two concepts to keep apart: monthly cash outflow (≈ $3,930, the money actually leaving your account) vs economic cost (interest + tax + insurance + maintenance + the opportunity cost of your down payment). The principal portion of your mortgage builds your net worth — it's not "spent." So "buy when the mortgage equals rent" is still a trap, but the trap is the overlooked carrying costs, not the mortgage itself.

Step 2: The True Cost of Renting

Say a comparable rental is $2,800/month. But don't forget: rents trend upward over the long run (actual increases vary by year, area and unit type, and Ontario rent control caps increases on many existing units). Renting costs you "as you go," while a mortgage payment is "locked in" only for the fixed-rate term — comparing a single month's numbers across the two is misleading.

The 5% Rule: A 30-Second Screen (Not a Decision)

A commonly used rough screen: annual rent ÷ home price. Well below 5% suggests renting deserves a serious look; well above 5% suggests buying is worth costing out in detail. But it's only a signpost for which direction to research next — not a substitute for a full cost comparison. Tax rates, interest rates, how long you'll stay, and your personal situation can all flip the answer.

Our example: $2,800 × 12 ÷ $650,000 = 5.17% — squarely in "run the detailed numbers on buying" territory, not a "you should buy" verdict.

Hidden Upsides of Buying

  • Principal paydown = forced savings: about a third of your payment builds equity; over 10 years that's substantial net worth
  • Leverage (works both ways): $130K down controls a $650K asset — but that's a before-cost illustration, ignoring interest, transaction fees and carrying costs. Leverage magnifies losses exactly as it magnifies gains
  • Temporary cost certainty: within a fixed-rate term (typically 3–5 years in Canada) your payment stays flat, hedging rent increases for that period — but the rate and payment can change at renewal. It's not locked in for life
  • Stability: no landlord selling out from under you or raising the rent

Hidden Upsides of Renting

  • Flexibility: job change, family change — move with proper notice to the landlord
  • Opportunity cost of the down payment: if $130K earned 6% annually (hypothetical illustration only, not a return promise), that's $7,800 a year
  • Zero maintenance headaches: leaky roof? The landlord's problem
  • Try before you buy: rent a year to learn the neighbourhood, then decide where to buy

5 Questions to Decide

  1. Will you stay at least 5 years? (Less than that, transaction costs eat the gains)
  2. Is your job stable for the next 3 years?
  3. Beyond the down payment, do you have 6 months of living expenses saved?
  4. Can you stomach a 10–15% short-term price swing?
  5. Do you truly know the neighbourhood? (If not, rent first)

All "yes" means you're in a good position to evaluate buying seriously — have a professional run the specific numbers with you. Any "no" → renting while you save and learn is usually the steadier choice. This is a starting point for self-assessment, not a substitute for advice tailored to your finances.

The Bottom Line

There's no universal answer — only the right answer for you. The key is doing the full math, not going with your gut.
